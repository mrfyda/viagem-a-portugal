Ten days, in early June, heading always north. This was the second leg of retracing Saramago's *Viagem a Portugal*, and quite possibly our favourite yet. It was above all a journey of villages and mountains: the first corn-cribs we'd ever seen, cows strolling through the middle of streets, people still herding livestock by hand, and stone, stone everywhere. The weather threw everything at us, days of sun and days of thunder, and at one point we had to cross into Spain and back simply because there was no other way through. Saramago warns that much of these journeys is lived on the road itself, more than in the towns, and it's quite true. The cities, almost all of them, we left quickly. It's the villages that stayed with us.

## Day 1 — Saturday, 3 June

_The day's route: Fátima, Arouca._

We set off north. We passed through Fátima halfway there, barely stopping, and went to sleep in Arouca.

### Fátima

> «Hoje, a imensa esplanada é um deserto»
>
> — José Saramago, *Viagem a Portugal*

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-2/fatima.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Fátima" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>The sanctuary, seen from above</figcaption>
</figure>

We stopped only briefly in Fátima. We came in from above, through the arcades, and that's the part that stayed with us: from there you can see the whole esplanade laid out below and get a sense of the scale of the thing. It isn't beautiful, or at least beautiful isn't the first word that comes to mind, but it's impressive, and we weren't expecting to find it interesting. Down below it's just a vast grey square, where the architectural details are lost. Neither of us had been before and we hadn't remembered it being so big. Most things were closed, so we stayed outside.

There were pilgrims, some fulfilling a vow on their knees, and there's the Cova da Iria, where Our Lady appeared. To us nobody appeared, neither Our Lady nor the little shepherds. We took a turn around, saw the sanctuary and moved on. Saramago writes that the immense esplanade is a desert. To us it appeared full of people.

### Arouca

> «terem passado naquele mesmo instante três belíssimas raparigas, altas, esbeltas, seguras, que pareciam doutro tempo, passado ou futuro»
>
> — José Saramago, *Viagem a Portugal*

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-2/arouca.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Arouca" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>A stone house, with a cross</figcaption>
</figure>

We slept in Arouca, at the house of a man who had a vegetable garden and a yard full of wild things. He was the one who took us to see the garden, and that's where we saw raspberries growing for the first time in our lives. The two of us just stood there staring at them, as if they were a tourist attraction.

For dinner, we asked a man on the street where one ate well. He pointed us to a restaurant we'd already spotted from outside, and off we went. It was one of those classic Portuguese places, fancy in the Portuguese way: gentlemen in white shirts, the air of a seafood house, and that somewhat distant savoir faire you almost never find any more (but they were kind). We ate far more than we needed and stayed nearly three hours, a good part of it just waiting to be allowed to pay the bill.

Arouca's centre is pleasant. Not much happens there, but it feels like a place you could live. Today it has the boardwalks and the nature park, so there's some nature tourism, and it still receives pilgrims, without whom there would be almost no tourism at all.

## Day 2 — Sunday, 4 June

_The day's route: Arouca, Vale de Cambra, Pedras Parideiras, Capela da Senhora da Lage, Marco de Canaveses, Amarante._

From Arouca we climbed up to the Serra da Freita, came down to Marco de Canaveses to see Siza's church, and ended the day in Amarante, in the middle of the festivities.

### Pedras Parideiras

_Not part of Saramago's journey: the Pedras Parideiras Interpretation Centre, in Castanheira, a geosite of the Serra da Freita (Arouca Geopark) where the granite slowly releases nodules of biotite._

  <figcaption>The "birthing stones"</figcaption>
</figure>

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-2/pedras-parideiras-2.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Pedras Parideiras" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>A corn-crib, the first of the journey</figcaption>
</figure>

It sits up in Castanheira, on the Serra da Freita plateau. The mother rock is an enormous boulder that keeps shedding tiny nodules, almost baby pebbles, which work themselves loose from the little hollows where they sat. It's called Castanheira nodular granite, found only in this part of the country, and apart from here there's just one other place in the world where it happens, near St Petersburg, in Russia. The locals, it should be said, aren't terribly interested in the geological explanation: for them there's something mystical about it, more pagan than religious — the stone gives birth to stone, and that's that.

There's a tiny interpretation centre and, outside, you can walk around looking at the stones and, with luck, catch one nearly being born. We didn't see many in plain sight. Absolutely nothing was happening: it was just us, and later a person or two showed up.

The hamlet is right up against the interpretation centre and amounts to three houses. There were some women cleaning, and just as we were leaving, the grocery van showed up honking. The old ladies all came out to buy things from him, onions and whatever else he was carrying, and we bought some cherries, already a bit old and soft, but it was worth it for the scene. There isn't a single supermarket there, and the place is far from everything: without a car, you don't live there.

This was also where we saw the journey's first corn-cribs, which one of us introduced to the other, with great conviction, as chicken coops.

### Capela da Senhora da Lage

_Not part of Saramago's journey: the Chapel of Nossa Senhora da Lage, in the Serra da Freita (Arouca)._

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-2/capela-da-senhora-da-lage.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Capela da Senhora da Lage" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>The chapel, alone on the mountain</figcaption>
</figure>

Another chapel at the top of nowhere, up on the Serra da Freita, on a mountain where there's nothing else but a cemetery next door. Saramago never came through here, and it's easy to see why: there's nothing to see, apart from exactly that, a chapel alone up there. It was a short stop, more for the strangeness of the place than anything else.

This was also where we went to see the Frecha da Mizarela, quite nearby, close to Albergaria da Serra. It drops some seventy-five metres in one go, the tallest waterfall in mainland Portugal and one of the tallest in Europe, fed by the Caima river. We stayed there a little while. The rest of the day was road, and we wanted to get down.

### Marco de Canaveses

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-2/marco-de-canaveses.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Marco de Canaveses" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>The altar, in Siza's church</figcaption>
</figure>

We went to Marco de Canaveses to see the Church of Santa Maria, by Álvaro Siza, the one church on this whole journey we truly made a point of seeing. Saramago passed through before it existed. It caused a great stir at the time and today is a landmark of the country's architecture.

We got something like a guided tour: the lady who was there talked with us and pointed out the details and all the symbolism, because inside nothing is by chance, it's all intentional. The side doors correspond to the twelve images of Christ, which aren't drawn anywhere but are counted in there all the same. The main door is gigantic, far taller than usual, and it's gigantic on purpose, to make us feel small on entering. And it's all stripped of gilding, unlike practically every other church we saw on this trip.

It's so bare that we joked it looks more like a multi-sports pavilion: take away two or three things and you could play basketball in it. It also struck us as almost ready to be a space for any religion, should that ever be needed. Next to it there's a house that used to be the sacristy, and what you actually visit is exactly that, the room where he got dressed, his wardrobe. It isn't a museum, it's the thing itself.

## Day 3 — Monday, 5 June

_The day's route: Amarante, São João de Gatão, Parada de Cunhos, Vila Real._

We woke in Amarante, with the Feast of São Gonçalo still winding down, and headed for Vila Real, with two short stops along the way.

### Amarante

> «vai o viajante entrando em Amarante, cidade que parece italiana ou espanhola, a ponte e as casas que na margem esquerda do Tâmega se debruçam»
>
> — José Saramago, *Viagem a Portugal*

</figure>

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-2/amarante-2.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Amarante" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>The church, on the other side of the Tâmega</figcaption>
</figure>

We reached Amarante at the end of the previous day and caught the city in the thick of the Feast of São Gonçalo. Everything was jammed. Our hotel was right in the centre; we had no idea what awaited us, didn't know who would take our car or how chaotic it would get, and still we managed to find a little parking spot and somewhere to eat, with vegan options.

The hotel was in a lovely, cosy building, in a spot where sweets were also sold, with a café out front serving local cakes. The room had one particularity: the bathroom door was glass, almost facing the bed. To one side, to be precise, but you could see everything. There was a curtain you could draw, something we only remembered afterwards. A person sometimes needs a little privacy. As for the decor, it was that style we've seen all over the country: gentrified Portuguese architecture, marble and gilding, the classic taste of the emigrant who came home. It's a socio-political comment about Portugal, not a complaint.

Of Amarante itself we kept a fond memory. It's a very pretty and very liveable little city, with a historic centre and houses leaning out over the Tâmega, a riverside town garden, local commerce and plenty of cultural life. Things were happening the whole time, cultural and sporting, and there are festivals with funny names where people open their doors for others to come in. Outside the historic centre it's less pretty, but the core is well worth the stop.

And there is, tucked in a corner, the Solar dos Magalhães, which at first we assumed was something much older than it is. It's what remains of a manor house that Napoleon's troops burned down in April 1809, the first house in the city to burn, and which was never rebuilt: the ruins were left standing, just as they were, as a memory of Amarante's resistance. When we passed by it was under restoration, following a Siza design to house the city's Casa da Memória.

### São João de Gatão

> «assim como não dirão que súbita comoção é esta que enche de lágrimas os olhos do viajante»
>
> — José Saramago, *Viagem a Portugal*

<figure>

We went there just to see the railway station. It's abandoned, but rather lovely, with all its colourful tiles, and there are stations still in operation in worse shape than that one. One of us had found it while planning the trip, and found it thinking of the other, who has a soft spot for abandoned stations, so the stop was more a gift than a visit.

By the station we saw a snake (one of us saw it, the other believes it). There's also a cemetery there. We never actually went into the village itself: if Saramago went there to see a church, we didn't see it — we really went only for the station.

### Parada de Cunhos

> «até Parada de Cunhos, e aí, voltando costas ao rio Corgo, enfrenta o Marão»

> — José Saramago, *Viagem a Portugal*

Of Parada de Cunhos we kept little, and there's no point pretending otherwise. It's a tiny little place, with people hanging out their washing and a church that, seen from a certain angle, looks like it's laughing. Looks like a duck. That was our great conclusion about Parada de Cunhos. We stopped, we looked, and we moved on, which is what happened in quite a few places on this trip: we passed through and stopped, or stopped and passed through, but at least we stopped.

We walked a stretch of boardwalk beside the river, which runs well below the city, since Vila Real sits up high. It was a bit poorly maintained and had a somewhat dystopian, abandoned feel: at one point we were walking through the undergrowth like something out of *The Last of Us*. Still, it made for an entertaining walk.

Afterwards we had the best vegan francesinha ever. We tried to get there on time, as always, and as always the restaurant was full, and we waited two hours for a table. It was worth it, and we left thrilled to have found a place with several decent vegan options in the middle of Vila Real.

We crossed the city on foot from one end to the other, from the cemetery on one side to the restaurant on the other, which gives some idea of its size. It's a small city, it was clean and tidy (and being clean is a good thing), and here too there were festivities going on. There weren't many people on the street at that hour. Of Vila Real's church, which apparently exists and is presumably a fine church, we kept no memory at all.

## Day 4 — Tuesday, 6 June

_The day's route: Vilarinho de Samardã, Vila Pouca de Aguiar, Boticas, Pero de Lagarelhos, Chaves, Montalegre, Pitões das Júnias, Outeiro._

The busiest day of the trip, and the first with bad weather looming. From Vilarinho de Samardã we climbed up into the Barroso, through Vila Pouca de Aguiar, Boticas and Montalegre, all the way to the far reaches of Pitões das Júnias, and went to sleep in a hamlet of three houses. Along the way there were places we didn't get to see: the Marão, which we crossed through the tunnel without stopping, and Covas do Barroso.

### Vilarinho de Samardã

> «Ainda teve o viajante tempo para ouvir o zumbido das abelhas e seguir ao longo da casa, espreitando as varandas corridas»
>
> — José Saramago, *Viagem a Portugal*

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-2/vilarinho-de-samarda-1.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Vilarinho de Samardã" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>The corn-cribs, in the middle of the village</figcaption>
</figure>

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-2/vilarinho-de-samarda-2.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Vilarinho de Samardã" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>A corn-crib, leaning against a house</figcaption>
</figure>

Vilarinho de Samardã and neighbouring Samardã were among the prettiest places of the day: two tiny villages, very old and a little neglected, practically next door to each other. We passed through both, one after the other, and to this day we're not sure what happened in which. In Samardã we stopped and walked around the village, which was perhaps the prettier of the two. In Vilarinho we pulled over by the roadside, in front of a school, and walked to see the house.

The house belonged to Camilo Castelo Branco, where he lived, still a teenager, between 1839 and 1841. His sister had married and he was taken in by his brother-in-law, the priest António de Azevedo, who gave him his first schooling, set him reading the Portuguese classics and learning Latin and French. This is where he came to know first-hand the rural life he would later tell of in his novels. You get there down some very, very narrow streets. The house has an inscription over the door, and the stream runs just a few steps away, so close it almost comes indoors. It's barely even a river, more a brook. The street is named after him.

Here the corn-cribs were already everywhere, in the middle of the village and leaning against the houses. The brooks began here too, and the feeling that we were finally in a different Portugal, unlike anything we'd seen up to that point. We're also left with the memory of a dog that decided our legs belonged to it. That annoyance aside, these were two very calm and very pretty places.

Before the town proper there's Parada de Vila Pouca, down in a valley, and that's where we stopped first. There was a little donkey, and obviously we went to make a fuss of it. You pass beneath a magnificent bridge, gigantic, three hundred thousand metres high (or so it felt).

Vila Pouca de Aguiar is already a fairly sizeable place, for what we'd been seeing, with a railway station also abandoned and a church. Honestly, it isn't memorable: it was more of a lunch stop. But the lunch, now that was something.

We drove around and stumbled on a place packed with people inside. We went to ask if it was open. It was that classic builders' canteen: everyone working nearby having lunch in the same place, with a set menu and home cooking, and the bill must have come to about six euros for two people. We weren't expecting it at all, and it was really good, home food and not more of the same we'd already been eating along the way.

### Boticas

_Not part of Saramago's journey: the town of Boticas, in the Terras de Barroso._

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-2/boticas.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Boticas" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>The church</figcaption>
</figure>

We'd been quite keen to see Boticas, who knows exactly why, and then we got there and there wasn't much to see. The town itself boils down to the centre, which we saw in no time.

What saved the stop was the Nadir Afonso Arts Centre. It's a contemporary building that, at first glance, looks utterly out of place in a town that size, and it holds a fine collection of works by the painter, who had family roots in Boticas on his mother's side. It's a good museum. It was hot, and we spent a good while in there, which made for a nice moment to rest and take in a bit of art. Covas do Barroso, right next door and something we also wanted to see, went unseen.

Montalegre stuck with us because of the water. It has its own sparkling water which, as far as we could tell, is practically only sold around there, and we discovered it by chance: we walked into a little café at the far end of town (there were three cafés) and that's what they served us. It was actually good. Some say it's the best sparkling water in the world, and we're not about to argue. Must be a lack of production to reach the rest of the country.

We confess we spent half a recording arguing over whether the water was from Montalegre or from Melgaço, and settled on Montalegre. The town is pretty, and has the castle up on the height, once the Braganças', which requires a bit of a climb up to it, longer than it looks from below.

### Pitões das Júnias

_Not part of Saramago's journey: the village of Pitões das Júnias and the Monastery of Santa Maria das Júnias, in the Peneda-Gerês National Park._

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-2/pitoes-das-junias.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Pitões das Júnias" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>The ruins of the convent</figcaption>
</figure>

Getting to Pitões das Júnias is already an adventure, and not because of the road, which is reasonable enough: it's because of the cows, which occupy the way and are in no hurry at all. The place is a dead end, tucked almost against the border, and on the other side there's nothing, not even a road.

There are two versions of the village, and both are ours. One says you arrive and nothing happens, that there's nothing to see, that it's farmers and livestock people living there for some reason. The other says the place had plenty of life: school vans stopping, several people out on the street. Both are right. What's beyond dispute is the view, enormous, over the plains of the Gerês.

And then there's the Monastery of Santa Maria das Júnias, which we found almost by accident. There was a sign saying "convent" and we went, convinced it would be right there by the road. It wasn't: we had to leave the car up top, because there was no going further by car, and climb down over some rough stones, some little rocks. And suddenly it started to rain. We argued a lot about whether it was actually raining at that moment, and the photographs say it wasn't, but at least one of us is certain it was.

The monastery is all in ruins, obviously, and quite beautiful and interesting. It's an almost mystical place. It was one of the things that struck us most on the whole trip, mostly because we can't quite explain how we ended up there. It remains one of the great mysteries of our Google Maps.

_Not part of Saramago's journey: the village of Outeiro, on the Mourela plateau (Montalegre), inside the Peneda-Gerês National Park._

  <figcaption>A cow coming down the stone street, by the corn-crib</figcaption>
</figure>

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-2/outeiro-2.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Outeiro" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">

</figure>

We went to sleep in Outeiro, which is one of those names there are thirty thousand of in the country, and this was one of them. Here too there were cows, but these ones actually strolled through the middle of the village.

The village is three houses, or barely more, and they all belonged to the same family: the house where we stayed, the house for keeping the cows, and the smokehouse. That plus a church and that's just about the whole village. There were two satellite dishes, which we counted.

When we arrived, the lady was bringing in the cattle and let us go and see where the cows slept. She was the mother of the woman who ran the guesthouse, and had met us halfway there and had been chatting with us ever since. She told us along the way: the house had belonged to her father or grandfather, she'd emigrated at some point, and now it belonged to her daughter. It was all in the family.

It's a Minho village through and through, all stone and cobbles, with water running along the edges of the road. We slept in a tiny wooden igloo and, at some point during the night, thunder started and it began to pour. The next morning we woke to sun and a beautiful day, with the view from there looking out over the river and the dam.

## Day 5 — Wednesday, 7 June

_The day's route: Vieira do Minho, São Bento da Porta Aberta, Covide, Ponte da Barca._

A day of coming down through the Minho, from Vieira do Minho to Ponte da Barca, almost entirely by car. Vieira do Minho isn't exactly a pretty place, and its glory, for us, is being the home town of the great Toninho dos Frangos. It has the newspaper's offices, with the calendar always on display, and we didn't keep much more than that. We passed by the Ponte da Misarela and through Frades, stopped at a river beach along the way, and on those little roads there was an impressive number of people cycling and out on hikes.

Towards the end of the afternoon we passed through Terras de Bouro, where we stood in the main square and nothing was happening. We tried to find a river beach that was half under construction and wasn't up to much. One of the difficulties of this trip is exactly this: waterfalls and river beaches are hard to find even with GPS, and there were waterfalls we tried to see and never managed to reach at all.

### São Bento da Porta Aberta

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-2/sao-bento-da-porta-aberta.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="São Bento da Porta Aberta" width="1200" height="1600" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>The sanctuary, over the river</figcaption>
</figure>

Neither of us had ever heard of São Bento da Porta Aberta, and it turned out to be one of the biggest surprises of the trip. The place is practically a conference centre for religious people, and we didn't immediately understand what it was doing there. It's an enormous sanctuary, a sort of Fátima in miniature, on the Camino de Santiago, with hotels, endless parking and lots of people arriving.

The church is built right at the top, over the rocks, across several levels, and the view from up there is incredible, facing straight out over the river and what looked to us like a dam too. We were impressed, more by the scale and by never having known it existed than by anything else. Whoever goes to mass there presumably knows about it. And, on our way out, we saw deer nearby, which isn't something you'd expect to find at a sanctuary.

### Covide

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-2/covide.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Covide" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>The sign with the town's name, at the entrance</figcaption>
</figure>

We passed through Covide but didn't stop, except for the time it took to take the photograph. Saramago didn't linger here either, and our only proof of having been in Covide is a photograph of the sign at the entrance to the town, with the name written out. In our defence, the name lends itself to easy jokes, and we made every one of them.

### Ponte da Barca

> «chegando a Ponte da Barca, decidiu que não havia de deixar abater-se por desânimos e avançou para a serra do Soajo»
>
> — José Saramago, *Viagem a Portugal*

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-2/ponte-da-barca-1.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Ponte da Barca" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>The bridge, in the mist</figcaption>
</figure>

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-2/ponte-da-barca-2.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Ponte da Barca" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>At night, the toll booth at the end of the bridge, with the church behind</figcaption>

At some point on this trip it starts getting hard to tell the river-and-arcade towns apart, because they're all alike. That said, Ponte da Barca was the best of them, and there's not much argument there.

It's alvarinho wine country, and mist country, and we caught a mist that was no ordinary mist, it was genuinely incredible. It has the whole riverside stretch, it has the bridge, and the centre is very pretty, really very pretty, with plenty of life. We managed a vegan meal here, which helps. This is where we slept that night.

We'd already heard of Ponte da Barca, and we still came away enchanted, above all because of the mist. The photograph shown here, to be honest, isn't the best one we have of it.

## Day 6 — Thursday, 8 June

_The day's route: Giela, Arcos de Valdevez, Ermelo, Anta do Mezio, Soajo, Lindoso, Castro Laboreiro._

From Ponte da Barca we went on through Giela and Arcos de Valdevez, entered the Gerês park through the Porta do Mezio, and climbed up through Soajo and Lindoso.

Lindoso was, going in, meant to be the spot for corn-cribs, the place with a whole field full of them. We got there and a fierce downpour was coming down, the kind where you can't see a hand in front of your face. We pulled up by the corn-cribs and stayed there, in the car, waiting for it to ease off. It didn't. There was no getting out of the car, no taking photographs, nothing at all. When it seemed to slacken a little, we set off, because we had to reach Castro Laboreiro, where we were sleeping. It kept pouring the whole way.

And to reach Castro Laboreiro we had to cross into Spain and back out again, because there was no way through inside the country. We did it in a great deal, a great deal, of rain.

### Giela

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-2/giela.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Giela" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>The medieval tower</figcaption>
</figure>

In Giela we stopped at the Paço, which sits on a rise over the Vez river, with its sixteenth-century residential wing, Manueline windows, and the medieval tower beside it. The tower has been turned into a museum, with three floors devoted to the archaeology of the municipality, the history of the monument itself, and the Encounter of Valdevez, of 1141, when Afonso Henriques faced off against his cousin Alfonso VII of León and Castile. A good stop, and one of the few where we actually went inside to see something.

### Arcos de Valdevez

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-2/arcos-de-valdevez.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Arcos de Valdevez" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>The town hall</figcaption>
</figure>

Arcos de Valdevez was our biggest disappointment of the trip, and it has to be said plainly, because we'd heard plenty of people speak well of it, that it was pretty, that it had everything. It's already very much a city. The riverside area has been turned into a car park, and the rest is heavily built up, with little history in sight, as if they'd paved over everything.

The arcades are cute and there are pretty corners, but you have to work hard to see the pretty part of the town, and the rest is a bit ugly. Coming right after Ponte da Barca made it feel even worse. We both came away exhausted by it and left in something of a hurry.

### Anta do Mezio

_Not part of Saramago's journey: the Anta do Mezio, a megalithic monument (c. 3000 BC) beside the Porta do Mezio, the entrance to the Peneda-Gerês National Park at Arcos de Valdevez._

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-2/anta-do-mezio.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Anta do Mezio" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>The dolmen</figcaption>
</figure>

We stopped at the entrance to the Gerês park, at the Porta do Mezio, to see a dolmen. It's a megalithic monument some five thousand years old, right by the start of the Trilho dos Lobos trail. A short stop, one of those you make because the sign appears and curiosity wins out.

### Soajo

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-2/soajo.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Soajo" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>The corn-cribs, over the rock slabs</figcaption>
</figure>

Soajo was the first proper stop for corn-cribs. They all stand together, in the middle of the village, resting on enormous slabs of rock, a very old, almost ancestral image. It gives the sense of being in the deepest Portugal, and the views with the corn-cribs in front are very beautiful.

There's the Soajo dog, a hunting breed that was used by the royal family (King D. Carlos used dogs from the Serra de Soajo, as other kings had before him), and there's still an argument today over whether it's the same dog as the Castro Laboreiro one or whether they're two distinct breeds. People in Soajo hold very firm opinions on the matter. And there are the cachena cows, which we saw ladies walking through the village as if taking a dog for a walk on a lead.

Apart from that, not much happens there, but there are a couple of small restaurants, and that's where the day's spectacle unfolded: large groups of cyclists stopping for lunch. Two would arrive, then two more, and suddenly the place was packed with people eating in the middle of the mountains, going hard at the wine and beer, mug after mug, only to head back out and climb and descend mountains again for another fifty kilometres home. The restaurant must be well known, because there really were a lot of people there.

It's a small village, but with people in it: some standing at their doors, others walking back and forth. It was a good surprise, and one of the prettiest places of the trip.

### Castro Laboreiro

> «Castro Laboreiro chega sem avisar, numa volta da estrada»
>
> — José Saramago, *Viagem a Portugal*

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-2/castro-laboreiro.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Castro Laboreiro" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>The Roman bridge</figcaption>
</figure>

Castro Laboreiro was one of the high points of the trip, and it ranked in the top two.

We arrived soaked, the rain just about stopping, and stayed at a guesthouse run by a very kind couple, city people who'd decided to come and live there. It was the husband who took us to a special spot to photograph the river and the Roman bridge, and it was also he who, all excited, wanted to show us the museum. And there we learned the thing that stayed with us most from this trip.

Castro Laboreiro has the *brandas* and the *inverneiras*: people move house according to the season, up to the higher ground in spring and summer, down to the lower village when the cold sets in. And they move with everything, furniture in tow, livestock in tow. At the time there were still some who did it, one family, or two women, very few by now. There are also the widows of Castro Laboreiro, who aren't actually widows: they dress in black, with the stockings and everything, in the traditional outfits we'd only ever known from books, and we saw them walking about the village.

Then there are the dogs. There's the Castro Laboreiro dog centre, one of the oldest breeds on the Iberian Peninsula, bred to defend flocks from wolves, which nearly went extinct in the seventies with depopulation and the end of shepherding. And, out on the mountain, there are packs of feral dogs, which aren't wolves: they're abandoned dogs now making their own lives out there. It's a known problem in these parts, because people assume it's wolves killing the livestock when, apparently, it's often the dogs.

And we had dinner in a hamlet of ten people in the middle of the mountains, at a restaurant that's appeared on RTP more times than most and gets recommended by every presenter going. It really was very good, and the portions were huge. From there you could see an incredible view, with little dogs wandering about the mountains.

The next morning we climbed up to the castle. The weather was still bad, a little better but still bad, with strong wind, fog and rain. It's high up, you climb steps to get there, and with the wet stone everything was slippery, so there were stretches where we had to lean against the wall and huddle against the wind. It didn't give access to everything and we didn't see all there was to see, but the view was enormous, rain and wind mixed in, and there was still a bit of a walk to be had around it.

## Day 7 — Friday, 9 June

_The day's route: Castro Laboreiro, Cevide, Melgaço, Monção, Valença._

From Castro Laboreiro we came down to the Minho, with a stop in Cevide, right on the border, and continued through Melgaço and Monção to Valença, the ugliest (and most city-like) town of the whole trip.

### Cevide

_Not part of Saramago's journey: Cevide (Cristoval, Melgaço), by Border Marker No. 1, at the mouth of the Trancoso river in the Minho, the northernmost point of Portugal._

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-2/cevide.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Cevide" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>The bridge into Spain</figcaption>
</figure>

This is where Portugal begins, or ends, depending how you look at it. Border marker number 1 sits at the mouth of the Trancoso river, in the Minho, and you reach it along a boardwalk, with a stretch of path through some rocks and boulders. There's a steep bank, there's water, and on the other side is Galicia: we'd take two steps and be in Spain, turn around and be back here.

This is where people used to cross, and it was one of the old smuggling routes. Coffee went across, and whatever else came to hand, and there were people arrested and chased by the police on both sides. Today there's a monument honouring the smuggler, and it's only right that there is, because these were lives of hardship.

It was this stop, and what we heard in Castro Laboreiro, that finally explained something that had puzzled us: why so much salt cod and octopus is eaten in the northern interior, so far from the sea. Octopus dries, and dry it could travel, then be rehydrated. It passed from hand to hand, among acquaintances and families who supported one another, and that's how octopus became a typical dish and, in many parts of the north, the Christmas dish. A delicacy born of smuggling.

### Melgaço

> «a torre de menagem é coisa de tomo, avulta sobre o casario como o pai de todos»
>
> — José Saramago, *Viagem a Portugal*

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-2/melgaco.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Melgaço" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>The keep</figcaption>
</figure>

Melgaço is a typical old Portuguese town, plenty of stone about, and it has the keep that Saramago describes as the father of them all, towering over the rooftops. We came away with the impression of a pretty place.

What surprised us most was that it had quite a lot of life, even young people, which we hadn't seen in almost any of the other places: we'd been coming from very deserted, very abandoned towns. That said, the things there were to see were almost all closed, including a museum we couldn't get into but which had some very lovely doors. We walked a bit around the town centre and moved on, since this too was mostly a passing-through kind of place.

### Monção

> «Quando entra em Monção, acendem-se os primeiros candeeiros.»
>
> — José Saramago, *Viagem a Portugal*

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-2/moncao.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Monção" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>Inside the church</figcaption>
</figure>

Of Monção we remember little, and we've already discussed between ourselves what that says. We did pass through, that much is certain. We saw the church. There was another church, a castle and a river, and it's a fairly large town. It wasn't memorable, and we'll leave it there, which is more honest than making something up.

### Valença

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-2/valenca.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Valença" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>The fortress gate</figcaption>
</figure>

Valença managed to be the ugliest place of the whole trip, and managed it with some ease. It's like the worst of the Lisbon outskirts.

To be fair, and we do want to be fair, there's the part inside the walls, and that's actually pretty: an old, walled city, with life in it, not merely abandoned. It's very commercial and very touristy, and maybe that's why it didn't have much impact after a week spent looking at historic villages where nobody's around. But it is pretty. The trouble is that most of the city lies outside the walls, and that's frankly dreadful.

We stayed at a hotel a fair way from the centre. Eating wasn't easy either, since the restaurants are practically all inside the walls and, out here, there wasn't much. There's a bridge into Spain we wanted to walk across and never managed to do the whole thing: we looked at it and it seemed to be inviting us to turn back, since there was no margin at all between the cars and the pedestrians. On the far side, the Spanish city looked rather more liveable, with gardens and everything.

Even so, we came away with a very vivid memory of Valença, and we think it's precisely because it was so different from the rest: suddenly it was very much a city, and we weren't expecting it to be so city-like.

## Day 8 — Saturday, 10 June

_The day's route: Paredes de Coura, Rubiães, Romarigães, Ponte de Lima, Balugães, Abade de Neiva, Barcelos._

From Valença we came down through the Lima and Neiva valleys, through Paredes de Coura, Rubiães and Romarigães, to Ponte de Lima, and ended the day in Barcelos.

Paredes de Coura was small and big at the same time. We went to see the festival grounds, by the river, and it's quite a bit smaller than we expected, and sits well within the centre of the town. But what was happening there was something else: there was a fair, a market, plenty of people on the street, a music school with its students playing, and an international Lego exhibition, with people building whole scenes. That fair was a proper fair, the kind we'd expected to find all over the country on this trip and had barely seen anywhere, no markets, no street vendors. This one had everything, plenty of socks and plenty of clothes. It struck us as a lively place, even a young one, which in a country that sometimes seems half-dead is no small thing.

Through Balugães we climbed up to the Sanctuary of Nossa Senhora Aparecida, which sits at the top of the town, up some steps, a white church, and it was lovely. The best part, though, was the signs: at this site and throughout the sanctuary grounds it is forbidden to spend time playing games, playing profane music or courting. The unholy trinity, then, posted in several spots. We'd never seen signs like that anywhere else. There were also several monuments to the little shepherds, which you don't see much of either. It seems they tried to start a new Fátima here, apparitions and all, but it never caught on.

### Rubiães

> «Rubiães é um templo românico fechado. O adro está praticamente coberto de lápides sepulcrais, entre o antigo e o quase moderno.»
>
> — José Saramago, *Viagem a Portugal*

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-2/rubiaes.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Rubiães" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">

</figure>

We stopped in Rubiães because it's where Saramago stopped, and it's fair to say that was the only reason. It has a Romanesque church, which we found closed, with the churchyard practically covered in gravestones, people buried right at the church door, from the old to the almost modern. There's also something Roman about the place, a milestone or something of the sort.

The church isn't in the village itself, it's a little way out, and it's possible we only stopped there and not in the village proper. It was a short stop, and, to be honest, one of us no longer remembers Rubiães at all.

### Romarigães

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-2/romarigaes.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Romarigães" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>The private chapel, half hidden by the wall</figcaption>
</figure>

In Romarigães we stopped because of a house, and we didn't even manage to get inside it. It's the Casa Grande de Romarigães, belonging to Aquilino Ribeiro, who lived there and gave the house's name to a book. You only see a bit of the façade, and even the private chapel is half hidden behind a wall. We also passed the cemetery right beside it. It's a big house, but rather neglected today.

We walked around a bit: there's a wood around it good for a short walk, and at some point you come out somewhere unexpected. It's the sort of stop where you end up collecting leaves to press in books, and we're that sort of people.

### Ponte de Lima

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-2/ponte-de-lima.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Ponte de Lima" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>The bridge, with the Roman soldier on horseback</figcaption>
</figure>

After everything we said about Arcos de Valdevez, it has to be admitted that Ponte de Lima has exactly the same problem: a car park right on the riverbank, which ruins a good deal of the view. The difference is that in Arcos it was everything, the whole town built up around it, while here it's mostly the car park. It isn't an ugly place, but they've managed to spoil quite a lot of its best parts, and it's very touristy.

The historic centre is interesting and was rather well kept, and on the bridge stand statues of Roman soldiers, one of them on horseback, crossing the Lima as if it were still a Roman road. We spent most of our time on the other side of the river, in the riverside area, which was the tidier part, with restaurants and plenty of people out walking (must have been the weekend, or close to it). These three towns, Ponte da Barca, Arcos de Valdevez and Ponte de Lima, are often spoken of together because of the arcades and the river, and they really do have similarities. Had we seen them in the opposite order, Ponte de Lima might have charmed us more; coming right after Ponte da Barca, it was a little disappointing. Even so, it felt like quite a liveable place, probably a good one to live in, just not as pretty as the other.

### Abade de Neiva

> «Em Abade de Neiva foi o viajante ver o conjunto de igreja e torre de mais viva atmosfera medieval que até agora encontrou»
>
> — José Saramago, *Viagem a Portugal*

We went to Abade de Neiva to see the church, the one Saramago calls the most vividly medieval church-and-tower ensemble he'd found up to that point. It sits beside a cemetery and has its bell tower separate from the church's body, which gives it a different look.

When we got there a crowd of people were cleaning everything up and getting it ready for some feast, which happened at half the churches on this trip. That was mostly it: the church, the cemetery, and people tidying the square.

### Barcelos

> «nunca outro melhor manjar comeu nem espera vir a comer, porque não é possível repetir a inventiva humana esta maravilhosa e rústica comida»
>
> — José Saramago, *Viagem a Portugal*

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-2/barcelos-1.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Barcelos" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">

  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-2/barcelos-2.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Barcelos" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">

Barcelos, home of the rooster, closed out the day in style. We caught a concert there in the city centre, with musicians and loudspeakers, which happened to be rather dull, and we don't even know if it was a special occasion or just the norm around there.

Beyond that, it struck us as a genuinely interesting place, with plenty of artistic activity going on, the kind of town that piques your curiosity and where you could imagine living, or at least one well worth a proper visit. We went to the river beach, right there, and crossed the bridge to Barcelinhos, on the other side, which is a little less polished. We saw some ruins in the centre, right by our hotel, which weren't even a church's.

What struck us most was the life in it. On this trip we always turned in extremely early, and that day we headed back even in the afternoon and everything was wide open, full of people, and it stayed that way. Last day, already on the way home, with a stop along the way at the Roman ruins of Conímbriga.

## Epilogue

In the end, this became our favourite trip so far, and that's a rare consensus between the two of us. It took us furthest, into a Portugal of mountains and villages, richer in nature and in history than anything we'd seen before. What stays with us: the corn-cribs, the cows strolling through the middle of streets, people still herding livestock by hand, and that certain sense of community you can hear, even in the emptiest places, in conversations at the door.

Both of us liked the first part better, the upper stretch. As we came down towards the cities, everything grew less distinct and less unique, and there's something worth saying plainly here: most of the country's fallen cities are ugly. We passed through them quickly, and even where they have good things to offer, it's hard to get at them, because they aren't set up for visitors from outside. That, in itself, isn't necessarily bad. It just makes it harder to find what's interesting there.

There's also something we have to confess: it's hard to say we went to the Gerês. We were in the Gerês area, we saw its great stretches of green, hillsides full of real greenery and not just eucalyptus and stunted pines. But we didn't go hiking, didn't visit the waterfalls or the streams, didn't do any of what people actually go there to do. The park is enormous. That's for another time.

And, along the way, we learned that this country is only dull for those who pass through quickly. Saramago's book, too, can seem like nothing much happens in it, and that's exactly the point: almost everything worth having on this trip happened on the road, not in the towns themselves. Castro Laboreiro, Pitões das Júnias and Ponte da Barca stay at the top of the list, along with the certainty that there's a great deal left to see in those tiny places where nothing happens and everything is there.

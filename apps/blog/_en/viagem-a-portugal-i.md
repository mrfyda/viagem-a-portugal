## The journey before the journey

_Saramago's words are quoted in the original Portuguese throughout — it is the
book's language, and the map's._

We stumbled on José Saramago's *Viagem a Portugal* by accident, on RTP, through
the [series of the same name](https://www.rtp.pt/play/p10829/viagem-a-portugal)
presented by Fábio Porchat. The book was first published in 1981, after
Saramago drove across the country between October 1979 and July 1980 at the
invitation of Círculo de Leitores. We're quite fond of Saramago, but we had no
idea this book existed; curious, we watched the first episode, in which Fábio
Porchat visits, among other places, Torre de Moncorvo, Amarante and Chaves.
Watching it, we thought it would be something to make our own journey to
Portugal, following Saramago's footsteps 42 years later. Then someone gave us
the book, we put the series on hold and started taking notes on every place it
mentions and… there are a lot of them! Saramago stops at every little chapel
that crosses his path, and uses it as an excuse to learn a bit more about the
people and the lands he passes through. That's one of the most curious things
about the book: the interest and the knowledge that Saramago, a convinced
atheist, shows for sacred art, and how he turns that interest into a way of
connecting with the people he meets along the road.

Preparing the trip and adding every point to the map made us realise this
would take real organisation, and a few concessions to Saramago's itinerary —
room for some places we wanted to see where he never stopped, or barely did,
and some spare time for walks in nature. It also became clear this is not a
journey for a weekend or a single summer, so we're doing it in stages. This
first leg was eight days, 18 to 25 February 2023, from the centre of the
country to the Trás-os-Montes northeast and back. One of us drove nearly the
whole way which, between mountain ranges and roads that never seemed to end,
was not always easy.

> «É preciso recomeçar a viagem. Sempre.»
>
> — José Saramago, *Viagem a Portugal*

We started in Coimbra, on a Saturday — which turned out to be a small
miscalculation: nearly everything was closed. Good for the city, perhaps, but
dull for anyone arriving from out of town wanting to wander. We stayed one
night. There was time to climb up and down all the hills (Coimbra spares
nobody's legs) and to peek into the university gardens and the Botanical
Garden. That was the day's best surprise — bigger and more beautiful than we
expected, if a little unkempt, with a lovely view over the city. We saw an
endless stream of buses go by, which surprised us for reasons we can't quite
explain, and we even found a few vegan options. We never made it inside the
library and plenty was left unseen, but it's a likeable city, and it leaves us
an excuse to come back. And on the walls, everywhere, street art — Coimbra has
heaps of it. We kept a piece by Ninas, a menstrual cup, and one painted phrase
worth carrying around: «deixa a xoxota respirar».

## Day 2 — Sunday, 19 February

_The day's route: Figueira de Castelo Rodrigo, Vila Nova de Foz Côa._

The first proper day on the road, with the first almond trees in bloom
appearing along the way.

### Figueira de Castelo Rodrigo

> «pelo arco que sustenta o coro, constituído por elementos de pedra em forma de S e considerado único no País.»
>
> — José Saramago, *Viagem a Portugal*

  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-1/figueira-de-castelo-rodrigo.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Figueira de Castelo Rodrigo" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">

We stopped for lunch and landed in an enormous restaurant, full with a coach
tour, half a dozen people serving — and still we were out quickly. On the way
out we passed a field of almond trees in flower. We also visited a small museum
about a battle which, as far as we understood, helped keep Portugal independent
from Spain. The castle of Castelo Rodrigo, a little out of the way, was left
unseen.

### Vila Nova de Foz Côa

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-1/vila-nova-de-foz-coa.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Vila Nova de Foz Côa" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>The Côa river valley</figcaption>
</figure>

Of Foz Côa we never actually saw the town: we stayed at the Côa museum, at the
end of the day. The engravings, down in the valley, were left for another time
— they were far, it was getting late, and we didn't fancy wandering around
there after dark. But the Côa valley, deep and beautiful, was well worth the
stop.

## Day 3 — Monday, 20 February

_The day's route: Torre de Moncorvo, Vila Flor, Mazouco._

A varied day: a town that received us after dark, a church we barely remember,
and an off-map detour that nearly finished us.

### Torre de Moncorvo

> «Quando o viajante entra em Torre de Moncorvo, já há muito tempo que é noite fechada»
>
> — José Saramago, *Viagem a Portugal*

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-1/torre-de-moncorvo.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Torre de Moncorvo" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>The old railway station</figcaption>
</figure>

Saramago entered Moncorvo in the dead of night, and with us it was much the
same. It was still daylight when we arrived, but darkness fell so fast it might
as well have been night. The town sits high up, a little apart from everything,
and the first impression was — let's be honest — a touch unnerving. We stayed
in an enormous manor house which, at night and half empty, looked straight out
of an old telenovela. The moment we arrived we ran into a well-known local
character, chatting with the few people still out on the street. We walked a
good while looking for a restaurant (there were some dubious-looking bars and
little else) and ended up dining on soup and gizzards that turned out to be
very good. The next morning, with the town in a different light, we walked
around the Largo da Igreja, where someone had taken the trouble to interlace,
one by one, every single tree.

this, where nothing much happens and all that remains is the photograph.

### Mazouco

_Not part of Saramago's journey: the rock engravings of Mazouco, the first
open-air Palaeolithic art site identified in Portugal, were only discovered in
1981, two years after he passed through._

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-1/mazouco-2.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Mazouco" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>The rock engraving, deep in the valley, by the river</figcaption>
</figure>

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-1/mazouco-1.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Mazouco" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>The valley, over the river</figcaption>
</figure>

Mazouco wasn't on Saramago's route, nor could it have been: the rock engravings
were only discovered in 1981, two years after he came through. Since we hadn't
gone down to the Côa to see the ones in the park, we reckoned this was more or
less on the way and worth the detour. "More or less" is a manner of speaking,
because it meant descending an enormous valley on questionable roads. We left
the car in the centre of the village (a single square, old houses side by side
with renovated piles, and almost only older people about) and did the rest on
foot. The way down to the engraving, facing the river, was short but steep. The
way back up — in heat unfit for February (there were wildfires around and a
thunderstorm threatening) and without a drop of water on us — very nearly
finished us. At the top, some gentlemen decided we were half mad for having
walked down, and sent us off with oranges from Mazouco: «as mais doces, não há
outra laranja igual a esta» — the sweetest, no other orange like it. They were
absolutely right; they tasted like something else entirely. Our only regret was
not bringing more.

## Day 4 — Tuesday, 21 February

_The day's route: Miranda do Douro, Sendim, Mogadouro, Vimioso._

One of the favourite days. We woke in Miranda do Douro and spent the morning
with the donkeys of Atenor, before carrying on through Sendim, Mogadouro and
Vimioso.

> — José Saramago, *Viagem a Portugal*

We reached Miranda towards the end of the day, in the cold. The city sits above
the Douro, with that yellow cliff face Saramago describes, now covered in
graffiti. The historic centre is pretty and still has some life in it, though
the newer buildings outside it are mostly charmless, as in so many Portuguese
towns. We stayed in a guesthouse with underfloor heating, an unexpected luxury
for the cold outside. It was also here that one of us fell ill, something that
stayed with us for the days that followed.

### Atenor

_Not part of Saramago's journey: the Centro de Valorização do Burro de Miranda
(AEPGA), in Atenor, which shelters and protects the Miranda donkey breed._

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-1/atenor.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Atenor" width="1200" height="1600" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>The donkeys, in the AEPGA paddock</figcaption>
</figure>

This was the favourite moment of the whole trip. We booked a visit to AEPGA and
spent the morning petting the donkeys and playing with them. There were many,
with names like Dom Quixote and Dulcineia — the latter pregnant — and even
newborn foals. They are wonderfully gentle animals, and leaving was hard.

### Sendim

> «este molho de vinagre que faz transpirar as maçãs do rosto e é cabal demonstração de que há uma felicidade do corpo»
>
> — José Saramago, *Viagem a Portugal*

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-1/sendim-2.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Sendim" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">

In Sendim we had lunch at Gabriela, a restaurant we discovered through
[Fernando Alves's *Viagem a Portugal* podcast](https://open.spotify.com/show/3cUwgfQlqDnvUSJCWQP4WF)
on TSF, which spoke of it often. The lady who made it famous had passed away,
but the family keeps the house and its spirit. We ate an enormous posta
mirandesa, the kind that leaves you unable to get up from your chair. Heavy,
but worth every forkful — and what Saramago says about the happiness of the
body fits this place rather well.

### Mogadouro

> «do alto do castelo se podem deitar contas ao trabalho dos homens e das mulheres deste lugar»
>
> — José Saramago, *Viagem a Portugal*

<figure>

From Mogadouro we kept above all the castle, up on its height, alone over the
lands, a few houses further down. We went looking for it, climbed up, and
little more. We met nobody, and truth be told not much else stayed with us.

### Vimioso

> «sentado nos degraus que dão acesso ao adro, o viajante ouve contar uma história da história da construção do templo»
>
> — José Saramago, *Viagem a Portugal*

<figure>

Vimioso was a name that rang a bell, a place we wanted to know but which, in
all honesty, left us with almost nothing. Another small town with its church,
where we stopped and moved on.

## Day 5 — Wednesday, 22 February

_The day's route: Guadramil, Rio de Onor, Bragança._

Another of the favourite days, now heading for the north-easternmost corner of
everything, up on the border, before coming down to Bragança.

### Guadramil

  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-1/guadramil-2.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Guadramil" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">

</figure>

Guadramil must have about ten inhabitants and, that day, they all seemed to be
outside: some men tinkering with their cars, some women sitting by their doors.
It's a place that feels genuinely beyond civilisation, with its little cobbled
lanes and a small river running through. One image stayed with us, and we
still regret it: a woman sitting on the far bank, watching the water. We wanted
to take her photograph, but we were too shy, and didn't.

### Rio de Onor

> «Dobrou-se uma curva, aparece entre as árvores um luzeiro de água, ouve-se um restolhar líquido sobre as fragas, e depois há uma ponte de pedra.»
>
> — José Saramago, *Viagem a Portugal*

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-1/rio-de-onor-1.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Rio de Onor" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-1/rio-de-onor-2.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Rio de Onor" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">

Rio de Onor is a village split in two by the border: you cross from Portugal
into Spain without noticing, still inside the same village. The Portuguese
side, the older one, has its houses leaning against the river and a stone
bridge, just like the bend Saramago describes. The Spanish side, right there,
is more modern, and even had a pharmacy — things this side did not. There was
hardly anyone to be seen, just a café and some vegetable gardens, but you could
tell people still live there. It makes you think that, on this side, the
nearest city is Bragança — far away, and hard to reach.

Bragança was the biggest city of the trip after Coimbra, and you could feel the
difference: it has a university, walkways along the river and, like Coimbra,
hills without end. Saramago also arrived here tired, and we understood him
perfectly. Around the city we came across a genet — one of Bordalo II's animal
sculptures made of rubbish — and the Georges Dussaud Photography Centre, where
we fell for the book *Trás-os-Montes*, full of old photographs of the region
(we hunted for a copy afterwards, but it's nearly all sold out). For dinner we
found Batoque, a small hidden restaurant specialised in mushrooms, with dishes
you could eat vegan — a rare thing in these parts. This is also careto country,
though the best-known ones, all straw and colour, are from Podence; Carnival
was close, but we never caught them.

## Day 6 — Thursday, 23 February

_The day's route: Bragança, Vilaverdinho, Mirandela, Carrazedo de Montenegro, Chaves._

A day of many kilometres and many stops, crossing Trás-os-Montes from northeast
to northwest, all the way to Chaves.

### Bragança

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/viagem-1/braganca-7.avif' | relative_url }}" alt="Bragança" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>Benches over the old rails</figcaption>
</figure>

In the morning, before setting off, we went to Bragança's railway museum, one
of the branches of the National Railway Museum, housed in the station — a
station without trains. It surprised us: big and well made, better than we
expected for a place the line no longer reaches. A good part of it is about the
city's fight not to lose the train, and then to get it back. There was no happy
ending: the motorway won, as it almost always does.

### Vilaverdinho

>

place where we saw a city's name spelled out in those big letters that are now
everywhere. The Nacional 2 was once our plan, before the Journey to Portugal
took us down roads it doesn't travel.

## Day 7 — Friday, 24 February

_The day's route: Chaves, Pedras Salgadas, Murça._

A morning still in Chaves and then south, stopping at the springs of Pedras
Salgadas and in Murça, home of the famous sow.

Pedras Salgadas wasn't on Saramago's journey but, unlike Mazouco, it could have
been: the waters have been worked since 1871, long before he passed. Today it's
a park with a spa and a museum, but in the early twentieth century it was an
elite resort, casino and all. Its peculiarity is the naturally sparkling
springs — rare things, and Pedras has several (at one point eight were
counted). In the old days each fountain treated a different ailment, and there
was even a ticket telling each visitor which water to drink and how many times
a day. At the museum we learned that the water used to travel by ox cart down
to the Douro and onwards by boat, and that of one of the founders not a single
photograph exists — which lends the whole thing a certain mystery. The park is
free to visit; you only pay for the museum, or to sleep in the tree houses.

In Murça stands the famous porca — a great stone sow on the main square that
even lends her name to a wine, Porca de Murça. What is the story of the sow?
Honestly, we have no idea, and we've already forgotten. If you're curious,
consider it a challenge: go look it up. The town has some life to it, with its
cooperatives and people on the street, even if it felt a little empty.

## Day 8 — Saturday, 25 February

_The day's route: Buçaco._

The last day, already heading back to the centre. The evening before we had
driven down to Luso, in the rain and the dark, on narrow roads thick with
traffic — a drive we do not remember fondly. Saramago passed through Buçaco,
but not through Luso, where we stayed.

Before entering the forest we drank Luso water at the fountain right beside the
hotel, where plenty of people were filling bottles. It tastes better there than
bottled, we promise. Then we walked the Buçaco gardens, and Saramago is right:
that forest demands every word there is. Even in February everything was
intensely green, full of water and a thousand different greens, and we got a
good hike in through the middle of it. We also saw the Buçaco Palace,
astonishing, now a hotel (an expensive one — we peeked at the prices).

## Epilogue

At the end, we were left with a bittersweet feeling. Saramago, in the book, is
welcomed with open arms: he strikes up conversation with everyone, and there is
always somebody with a story to tell him. We, all this time later, found above
all empty villages. Many of these places are nearly deserted now, drained by
emigration, and there were whole days when we barely saw a soul on the street.
The few real conversations we had were with the gentlemen of Mazouco and their
oranges — and perhaps that is our greatest regret: that more stories like that
didn't come along, the kind you tell afterwards. Perhaps the fault is partly
ours, too; we're not the best at striking up conversation with passers-by.

But on the whole we loved it. The roads were far better than we expected, and
there is a particular beauty to this border strip of Trás-os-Montes — empty, at
times reminding us of the Alentejo. The favourite moments remain: the donkeys
of Atenor, the oranges of Mazouco, Guadramil, Chaves. And the wish remains too:
to come back, and to keep travelling.
